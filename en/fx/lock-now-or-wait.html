Should we lock the FX rate now or wait?

These are two questions, and merging them is the expensive mistake. Locking resolves certainty: you know your number. Timing resolves price. The useful question is not lock or wait, but where today's price sits within the range that currency has traded in, and whether you have a window.

What is the actual problem?

Most companies resolve this by temperament rather than by policy. The cautious treasurer locks every time; the optimistic one waits every time. Neither looks at the entry price.

Waiting because you expect the rate to improve is speculation, and treasury teams are right to reject it. But locking without regard to where the price sits is not prudence either: it is a different way of ignoring the same information.

What is missing is a third option: a documented trigger that says when to execute, which can be defended afterwards.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Because the industry divides the field into forecasting, which serious treasurers avoid, and systematic hedging, which ignores entry price entirely.

Nothing occupies the middle: an objective, repeatable description of where today's rate sits in its own history. Without that, "wait" always sounds like a hunch, so the default becomes locking on whatever day the approval happened to land.

Facts

  • Waiting to hedge while hoping the rate moves your way is speculation, not strategy. A documented trigger is what separates the two. Alt21
  • Hedging tells you how to lock a rate, not when the timing is right to do so. XE
  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That is the range a lock date is chosen within. MTFX Group

What does locking solve, and what does it not?

Locking solves planning. If your contract margin is thin and an 8% move erases the profit on the deal, certainty is worth more than price. Lock, and lock early.

What locking does not solve is the price itself. A rate fixed at the top of its historical range is still an expensive rate, except now you are committed to it for the term. Protection does not improve the number; it freezes the number available that day.

How do you tell whether today's price is a good entry?

Compare today's value to the range that currency has traded in over recent months and years, not to yesterday's rate or this morning's headline. The question is positional: within what this currency usually trades at, is today high, middle or low?

Treend returns that as a 0 to 100 reading, comparing each currency against its own history across several timeframes. It does not forecast and does not promise outcomes. It gives the execution decision something auditable behind it.

Can we lock part and leave part open?

Yes, and for most companies that is the correct answer. Lock the portion that needs certainty, the part covering cost and contracted margin, and keep flexible the portion that can wait for a better window.

Each part then has its own criterion, instead of one decision applied to everything.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Locking on the headline. By the time the news reaches you it is in the price.
  • Treating a lock as a decision without a price. Protection has a cost, and entry level is part of it.
  • Waiting with no written rule. Without a trigger, waiting becomes drift until the date forces your hand.
  • One decision for the whole exposure. Part of it needs certainty and part does not.

Frequently asked questions

Does Treend forecast the rate?

No. It reports where today's price sits within that currency's own history, as a 0 to 100 reading.

Is this not just market timing?

It is not a directional call. It describes the current level relative to history, which is what makes it documentable rather than speculative.

What if we must execute today?

Then there is no timing decision. Put the effort into the spread, which is still negotiable.

Does it cover euro, pound and yuan?

Yes, each currency read against its own history.
