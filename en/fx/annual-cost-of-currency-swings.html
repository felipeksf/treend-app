What currency swings cost you over a year

Multiply your annual foreign-currency volume by the average gap between the days you chose and the range available. A company moving US$ 1 million a year, in a currency that moves 2% to 5% across months, has tens of thousands of dollars exposed purely to the choice of dates.

What is the actual problem?

Currency movement is consumed as daily news. Up today, down tomorrow, each move with its headline. Framed that way it looks like something that happens to the company rather than something it manages.

A CFO does not live the day, though: they live the year. And across the year the sum of twelve mediocre decisions is a large number, which never appears consolidated anywhere because each transaction was booked in isolation at its own rate.

The missing report would say: last year we converted this much, and the dates we chose cost us this much against the range available.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Because consolidating that cost requires a historical reference per transaction, and finance systems do not store it. They store the contracted rate, not the range that existed in the window.

The news cycle does not help either: it treats movement as macroeconomics, with explanations about rates and policy. None of that converts into a line a finance team can use.

Facts

  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That is the annual amplitude planning must absorb. MTFX Group
  • Every extra day between contract and settlement enlarges the period in which an adverse move can occur. The exposure compounds across the year. MarketInvoice
  • A defensible FX process rests on documented triggers rather than directional feelings. Measurement is what makes the annual number real. Alt21

How do you build the number for your business?

Take annual converted volume in foreign currency. Multiply by a conservative estimate of the gap between your average day and a better available one: 1.5% to 2% is reasonable, and you can derive your own figure from the year's transactions.

At US$ 500,000 a year, 2% is US$ 10,000. At US$ 3 million, US$ 60,000. That is not guaranteed loss or promised gain: it is the size of what is exposed to date selection, and therefore the ceiling of what better process can address.

What does the number change in practice?

It changes priority. A director seeing "US$ 60,000 exposed to date selection" treats the subject differently from one seeing twelve loose transactions on a statement.

And it grounds the decision about how much to invest in the problem: tooling, process, or a dedicated person. If the number is small, the honest answer is to invest nothing, and knowing that is worth something too.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Treating movement as news. What matters is the annual total against your volume.
  • Using the worst case. Estimate with a conservative, checkable percentage.
  • Separating spread from date. Total FX cost is both.
  • Not repeating the calculation. Without year-on-year comparison there is no evidence of improvement.

Frequently asked questions

Is that figure a certain loss?

No. It is exposure, not loss. It measures how much date selection can influence, in either direction.

Does Treend guarantee recovering it?

No. There is no promised return. It supplies the reference; the result depends on your window and discipline.

How often should we recalculate?

Once a year at close, plus a mid-year review if volume shifts materially.

Does it work across several currencies?

Yes, calculating each against its own history and summing at the end.
