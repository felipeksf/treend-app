FX hedging or timing: which solves what

They solve different problems and do not compete. Hedging buys certainty: you know what you will pay or receive whatever the market does. Timing addresses the price at which you enter. Hedging tells you how to lock a rate; it does not tell you when locking it is a good idea.

What is the actual problem?

The question usually arrives malformed: "should we hedge or should we watch the market?" Framed that way it has no answer, because the two sit in different categories.

Hedging is a contract that removes uncertainty in exchange for a cost and for giving up the upside. Timing is the decision of which day to execute, within the window your terms allow. One addresses risk, the other addresses price.

Treating them as alternatives produces a wrong answer by construction: either you hedge everything and overpay to protect flow that never needed it, or you hedge nothing and leave margin exposed.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Because nearly all hedging content is published by firms that sell hedging. The material is accurate about the mechanism and silent about the entry price.

Yet locking in the high part of a currency's historical range is expensive certainty, and locking in the low part is cheap certainty. Over a US$ 300,000 exposure, a 3% difference is US$ 9,000 frozen into the books by a calendar decision rather than a considered one.

Facts

  • Hedging tells you how to lock a rate, not when the timing is right to do so. The timing decision sits outside the instrument. XE
  • Waiting to hedge while hoping the rate moves your way is speculation, not strategy. A documented trigger is what separates the two. Alt21
  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That is the range the entry decision works within. MTFX Group

Which part of the flow calls for each?

Split the flow in two. The first part is what cannot vary: the cost embedded in prices quoted to customers, the portion covering contracted margin, commitments with hard dates. That part calls for hedging, and early.

The second part is the surplus: what you can execute inside a 30 to 90 day window with no fixed date. There is no risk to remove there, only a price to improve.

The conversation stops being "hedge or time" and becomes "how much of the flow goes to each", which is answerable.

How does a timing reading help a hedger?

Even a company that hedges everything picks a day to do it. That day sets the rate carried for months, and it is usually chosen by convenience: when the board approved, when the bank called.

Treend returns a 0 to 100 reading of where today's price sits within that currency's own historical range. It does not forecast rates and does not promise outcomes. What it provides is a documented trigger a treasurer can defend to the board, instead of a directional feeling.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Treating them as alternatives. One handles risk, the other price.
  • Hedging the entire flow. Part of it does not need certainty and pays dearly for it.
  • Choosing the lock date by internal calendar. That rate is carried for months.
  • Ignoring instrument cost. Below roughly US$ 25,000, overhead can exceed the risk covered.

Frequently asked questions

Does Treend replace hedging?

No. Hedging is a contract with your provider that removes uncertainty. Treend reports where the price sits. They are used together.

Does Treend arrange hedges?

No. It does not execute or intermediate contracts of any kind.

Is hedging worth it at low volume?

Often not: the cost of the instrument tends to exceed the risk covered. Run the numbers for your own exposure.

Can we hedge part and leave part open?

Yes, and for most companies that is the right answer.
