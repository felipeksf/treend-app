Does your company convert currency well?

Compare each executed trade against the range of rates available inside your decision window. If your fills land consistently in the worse third of that range, money is being lost to the absence of a rule. Without that comparison, the question cannot be answered at all.

What is the actual problem?

Almost no company can say whether it converts well. It knows how much it converted, at what rate, and what it paid in fees. It does not know whether that rate was good, because nothing was ever compared against it.

This is an odd blind spot, because the same company measures everything else: days sales outstanding, freight cost per kilo, margin per product. FX, often the largest swing item of the month, goes unassessed.

What is not measured does not improve. A company can be filling in the worst third of the range for three years running and never find out.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Because the benchmark does not exist in any system. Your records hold the contracted rate; they never hold the range of rates that existed between the date you could have executed and the date you had to.

Treasury literature compounds this by treating FX data as an accounting problem: consistent rates across ERP, consolidation and reporting. Accurate books, no judgment of execution quality. The question "did we execute well?" appears in neither the manuals nor the tooling.

Facts

  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That spread is what a fill should be judged against. MTFX Group
  • Every extra day between contract and settlement enlarges the period in which an adverse move can occur. The window is the unit of analysis. MarketInvoice
  • A defensible FX process rests on documented triggers rather than directional feelings. Measurement is what makes a trigger reviewable. Alt21

How do you build the assessment?

List the last twelve months of trades with date, amount and effective rate, meaning the rate actually realized including spread and fees. For each, define the decision window: from when you could have executed to when you had to.

Then classify: inside that window, did the fill land in the better third, the middle, or the worse third? Count how many fall in each bucket.

An even spread means you are at chance, which already beats the worst case. Concentration in the worse third means a pattern, and patterns have causes, usually the habit of executing at the deadline.

What do you do with the result?

If the problem is real, the fix is a written rule followed consistently, not an attempt to predict the market. Something like: execute when the price sits in the favorable part of the range, inside the window, and accept the deadline when no opportunity appears.

Then measure again next quarter using the same method. That is how you learn whether the change worked, rather than relying on impression.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Benchmarking against the following day. The reference is the range across the whole window.
  • Using the screen rate. Judge by the effective rate, fees included.
  • Assessing a single trade. The pattern only shows across the year.
  • Changing the rule every month. Without stability there is nothing to evaluate.

Frequently asked questions

Can this be done without a tool?

Yes, with a spreadsheet and rate history, if trade count is low. It becomes impractical as volume grows.

Does Treend produce the assessment automatically?

It supplies the 0 to 100 reading that serves as the benchmark. You build the classification of past trades from it.

Is this forecasting?

No. It is assessment of the past and position in the present.

How many months should we review?

Twelve gives a reliable picture. Fewer and the result blends into the luck of the period.
