When should an exporter convert the proceeds?

Between shipment and conversion there is usually a window of weeks or months. Most exporters do not use it: they convert when cash is needed. Inside that window the chosen day changes what you keep, and the choice should come from where the price sits in the currency's own range.

What is the actual problem?

The goods shipped and the invoice amount is fixed. What is not fixed is how much of it becomes home currency, and that depends on a single day you choose.

Most companies do not choose it. Finance converts when the money is needed, or when the bank calls. The largest remaining variable in the deal gets settled by convenience.

It is not marginal. Rates commonly move 2% to 5% across months, and every extra day between contract and settlement widens the period in which an adverse move can happen. On a US$ 200,000 shipment that is thousands of dollars decided by cash-flow sequencing.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Search for the best time to convert export proceeds and you mostly find accounting: which rate to use for the books, average rates by period, how to report. That content is abundant because accountants produce it.

The execution question has no owner. It is not the accountant's remit, and the bank earns on the spread whether you convert well or badly. The exporter ends up deciding alone, with the screen rate and a feeling.

Facts

  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That is the range the conversion date works within. MTFX Group
  • Every extra day between signing a sales contract and receiving the funds enlarges the period in which an adverse rate move can occur. MarketInvoice
  • A defensible FX process rests on documented triggers rather than directional feelings. Without one, the conversion date is set by whoever needs cash first. Alt21

What is your real window?

Your window has two edges: when the funds arrive and when you need home currency in the bank. The gap between them is the decision space, and it is usually larger than people assume.

Write both down before shipping, not after. A company that knows it can wait 60 days decides differently from one that assumes it must convert on arrival, and both decide better than one that never asked.

Holding the foreign currency in a currency account, where available, widens the window at little cost.

How do you choose the day inside it?

The question is positional, not predictive: within what this currency usually trades at, is today high, middle or low?

Treend returns that as a 0 to 100 reading, comparing each currency against its own history across several timeframes. It does not forecast rates and does not promise outcomes. It gives the conversion decision a documented basis instead of a feeling.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Converting the day cash is needed. Need sets the deadline, not the best day inside it.
  • Converting on the headline. By the time you read it, it is priced in.
  • Not knowing the window. Whoever assumes one day behaves as if they had one day.
  • Negotiating only the rate, never the spread. Spread is permanent and volume-sensitive.

Frequently asked questions

Does Treend predict where the rate is going?

No. It reports where today's price sits within that currency's own history.

Does this apply to service exports?

Yes. The timing decision exists whenever there is a gap between invoicing and conversion.

What if we need the cash immediately?

Then there is no timing decision. Focus on the spread, which is still negotiable.

Does it work for euro and pound?

Yes, each currency read against its own history.
