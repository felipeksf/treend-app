Euro, pound and yuan: the same problem

The decision is identical to the dollar's: compare today's price against that currency's own history, inside the window you have. What differs is that almost all FX guidance is written about the dollar, leaving companies paying in euros, pounds or yuan without a reference.

What is the actual problem?

A manufacturer buying components in Germany pays euros. An importer from Guangzhou pays yuan. A services firm billing London receives pounds. None of them sees themselves in articles about when to buy dollars.

So they improvise: read the dollar content and apply it to the euro. It half works, because the logic is the same. It fails where it matters, because each currency has its own range and its own level. The euro can sit low in its history exactly when the dollar sits high in its own.

Deciding euro by looking at the dollar is measuring with the wrong ruler.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Because search volume for the dollar dwarfs the rest, and content follows volume. Producing material on the yuan takes the same effort and reaches fewer readers, so almost nobody does.

The side effect is that companies with meaningful exposure in other currencies have no reference at all. The irony is that they need one more, not less: operating in a less-covered currency means less news noise and more dependence on data.

Facts

  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. Each pair has its own amplitude, and today's price is read against that pair. MTFX Group
  • The quoted rate, spread and fees determine the delivered amount. In less liquid currencies the spread tends toward the higher end. MarketInvoice
  • Exchange rate cost depends on both the rate obtained and the payment method. Method matters more in thinner currencies. MTFX Group

What changes from one currency to another?

The ruler. Each currency has its own range and its own history, and the reading must be made against itself. That is why Treend returns a 0 to 100 reading per asset, comparing each currency with its own past and never one currency with another. FX coverage is dollar, euro, pound and yuan.

Liquidity changes too, which affects the spread quoted. Yuan and pound transactions often carry a wider spread than dollar at the same provider, and that is negotiated separately.

What if you operate in several currencies?

Treat each as an independent decision, with its own window and trigger. The temptation is one rule for "the company's FX", but that forces the smaller currency to follow the larger one's rhythm.

In practice a company buying in euros and selling in dollars has two opposite decisions running at once, each against its own range. Merging them usually degrades both.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Reading the euro off the dollar. Different ranges, different levels.
  • Accepting default spread in a thinner currency. That is where the most room sits.
  • One rule for every currency. Each needs its own window and trigger.
  • Converting via the dollar without checking cost. Triangulation can pay two spreads.
