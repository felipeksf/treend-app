When to buy currency to pay an overseas supplier

Buying on the due date is a decision by default, not a choice. If there is a gap between invoice and payment, there is a window, and the day chosen inside it changes the cost. The useful criterion is where today's price sits in that currency's own range.

What is the actual problem?

The supplier invoice arrives with an amount and a due date. Accounts payable buys the currency on the due date, because that is the process. Nobody decided it; it became routine.

But invoice terms commonly run 30, 60 or 90 days. Across that gap the rate moved, often by several percent. The company paid the price of the final day of the window, which has no particular reason to be the best one. It is simply the day the system flagged.

For a regular importer this repeats a dozen times a year, always the same way, and never surfaces as a cost anywhere.

Why is this gap rarely addressed?

Advisers repeat that companies should not simply buy at the due date. The line appears in article after article. None of them says how to pick a different day.

The reason is structural: this content is published by banks, brokers and payment providers, who earn on the spread. What matters to them is that you execute with them, not which day you execute. The question "which day?" has no natural owner.

Facts

  • FX rates commonly move 2% to 5% over a period of months. That makes the purchase date material across a 30 to 90 day window. MTFX Group
  • The quoted rate, spread, transfer fee and receiving fee together determine the amount actually delivered. A favorable headline rate means little if the all-in cost is worse. MarketInvoice
  • A defensible FX process rests on documented triggers rather than directional feelings. Without a trigger, the deadline becomes the decision. Alt21

How do you choose the day inside the window?

First define the real window: from the date funds or facility are available to the due date, minus settlement days. It is shorter than it looks, and it needs to be written into the process.

Then the question is positional: is today's price high, middle or low within the range this currency has traded in? Treend returns that as a 0 to 100 reading, comparing each currency against its own history. It does not forecast and does not promise outcomes.

The rule shifts from "buy at the due date" to "buy when the price is in the favorable part of the range, inside the window, and at the deadline otherwise".

What if the supplier accepts early payment?

Then the window widens and a second calculation appears. Some suppliers discount for early or annual prepayment, and that discount adds to any currency benefit. Against it sits the cost of taking cash out of the business early.

Put both in the same model. It is common to approve on the commercial discount alone and discover later that the currency was bought at a poor level, cancelling the gain.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Buying at the due date out of inertia. It is the one day in the window nobody chose.
  • Confusing urgency with the deadline. The due date bounds the window; it is not the best day in it.
  • Comparing on the screen rate. Judge by the all-in delivered amount.
  • Operating with no written window. Without one, every decision defaults to the deadline.

Frequently asked questions

Does Treend say what the rate will do?

No. It reports the position of today's price within the currency's own history.

Does it work for euro or yuan payments?

Yes, each currency read against its own history.

What if payment runs automatically at the due date?

That flow sits outside the decision until the internal rule changes.

Does Treend execute the payment?

No. Execution stays with your bank or payment provider, at their spread.
