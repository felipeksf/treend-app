How Treend decides a currency is cheap or expensive

Treend compares each asset's current price with that asset's own history and returns a number from 0 to 100, across several timeframes. A high number means the price is unusually low relative to that asset's past; a low number means the opposite. The comparison is always of an asset against itself, never between different assets.

What does the 0-to-100 number mean?

It means where today's price sits inside the asset's own history. Near 100, the price is in a range that asset has rarely been in, which for a buyer is unusually cheap. Near 0, the opposite. The number is not a quality score: it says nothing about whether the company or the currency is good, only where the price is.

Why are there several timeframes?

Because the same price can be high over one period and low over another. A dollar at the top of last month's range may sit in the middle of the five-year range. Someone converting this week needs the short reading; someone deciding a yearly contribution needs the long one. Treend shows both, and disagreement between them is information, not an error.

How does Treend alert you?

You choose the asset and the range you care about, and you are notified when it enters that range. That is the difference between configuring once and checking a screen every day. The alert works both for a currency you need to buy and for one you need to sell.

Does Treend forecast prices?

No. It does not project what will happen and does not claim a price will rise or fall. All the reading says is where today's price sits relative to that asset's past. That is a statement about the present, verifiable against the history, not a statement about the future.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Reading the number as a score for the asset. It measures price against history, not quality.
  • Comparing the numbers of two different assets. Each reading is of an asset against itself.
  • Using one timeframe only. Disagreement between periods is part of the answer.
  • Waiting for the extreme. The range lasts days or weeks; the exact point is only identifiable afterwards.
